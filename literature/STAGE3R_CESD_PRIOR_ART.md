# Stage 3R C-ESD Prior-Art Kill Audit

Date: 2026-09-04
Candidate: **Endogenous Standard Differentiation × Strategic Product Repositioning**

## Verdict

Ingredient overlap is severe. No novelty claim is available from any of the following separately:

- government standard-recognition policy;
- government compatibility policy;
- standardization unions;
- endogenous product differentiation after regulation;
- compatibility plus network effects;
- network effects plus endogenous product positioning;
- redesign costs.

The only potentially surviving contribution is the full feedback

`government standards partition -> continuous bloc/recognition depth -> regime-specific strategic product repositioning -> national welfare -> standards-coalition stability`.

No audited close paper found in this pass contains that complete loop, but this is **not yet a novelty finding** because the continuous policy map under IS/SU/SW is still unresolved.

## 1. Ruiz (2004) — closest policy-to-positioning threat

Juan M. Ruiz, *Mix-and-Match and International Standardization Policy* (2004 working paper).

The paper studies governments' incentives to accept/reject foreign standards in differentiated-product industries. Its headline policy result is robust when firms' product characteristics are endogenized after governments choose recognition policy.

Threat to C-ESD:

- government policy precedes product-characteristic choice;
- firms strategically choose differentiation after observing policy;
- price competition and domestic-government welfare are analyzed.

Therefore C-ESD **cannot** claim novelty from `government standards policy -> endogenous product differentiation -> prices`.

Distance from C-ESD:

- binary foreign-standard recognition rather than continuous compatibility/differentiation depth;
- two-country mix-and-match setting rather than three-country IS/SU/SW partition stability;
- network externalities are not the core source of regime-specific repositioning;
- no government standards-coalition stability result generated by continuous policy depth and subsequent product repositioning.

Source: Ruiz (2004), available author manuscript / working-paper copy, *Mix-and-Match and International Standardization Policy*.

## 2. Jonard & Schenk (2004) — closest circle/compatibility/network threat

Nicolas Jonard and Eric Schenk, *A note on compatibility and entry in a circular model of product differentiation*, Economics Bulletin (2004).

They analyze horizontally differentiated network goods on a circular product space. Compatibility raises network utility but makes products closer substitutes, creating exactly the broad differentiation-versus-network tradeoff relevant to C-ESD.

Threat:

- circle geometry;
- network externalities;
- compatibility changes effective product differentiation;
- downstream pricing/entry consequences.

Important residual window:

Their model does not make product locations an explicit strategic choice responding to compatibility. The paper explicitly treats endogenous positioning that counteracts compatibility-induced differentiation loss as outside its scope.

Thus the firm-side C-ESD repositioning mechanism is not directly absorbed by this paper.

## 3. Gandal & Shy (2001) — mandatory three-country standards-union benchmark

Neil Gandal and Oz Shy, *Standardization policy and international trade*, Journal of International Economics 53(2), 363–383.

This is a mandatory threat because it already contains:

- three countries;
- government recognition of foreign standards;
- standardization unions;
- horizontal product differentiation;
- network effects and/or conversion costs;
- government coalition incentives.

Therefore none of those ingredients is novel.

Distance:

- government recognition is essentially discrete rather than a continuous standard-differentiation depth;
- firms do not subsequently choose Hotelling/Salop locations as a strategic response to the government standard policy;
- no `policy depth -> product repositioning -> coalition stability` interaction.

DOI: 10.1016/S0022-1996(00)00067-2.

## 4. Klimenko (2009) — mandatory continuous government compatibility benchmark

Mikhail M. Klimenko, *Policies and international trade agreements on technical compatibility for industries with network externalities*, Journal of International Economics 77(2), 151–166.

Klimenko already analyzes strategic government compatibility policies, network externalities, international duopoly/perfect competition, and international agreements. Equilibrium compatibility can be excessively high or very low depending on primitives.

Therefore `continuous compatibility + government policy + network effects + international coordination` is already occupied.

Distance:

- product-location choice is not the strategic continuation margin generating the government-policy feedback;
- C-ESD would require the standards partition to alter the product-positioning response and hence coalition participation/deviation incentives.

## 5. Baake & Boom (2001) — endogenous product differentiation plus compatibility/network effects

Pio Baake and Anette Boom, *Vertical product differentiation, network externalities, and compatibility decisions*, International Journal of Industrial Organization 19, 267–284.

Four-stage game:

1. firms choose inherent product qualities;
2. firms decide compatibility via adapter;
3. price competition;
4. consumption.

This directly shows that endogenous product differentiation and compatibility/network effects are already jointly modeled.

Distance:

- private compatibility choice, not government standards-union policy;
- vertical rather than C-ESD's horizontal product repositioning;
- no three-country government coalition stability.

DOI: 10.1016/S0167-7187(99)00029-6.

## 6. Wang & Lyu (2020) — endogenous horizontal positioning with network/compatibility tradeoff

Wei Wang and Gaoyan Lyu, *Sequential product positioning on a platform in the presence of network effects*, International Journal of Production Economics 229, 107779.

This paper directly studies endogenous product positioning when differentiation reduces compatibility and network effects matter. It derives closed-form positioning results.

Threat:

- endogenous horizontal product positioning;
- network effects;
- explicit tradeoff between product differentiation and compatibility.

Hence C-ESD cannot claim novelty from the firm-side tradeoff itself.

Distance:

- private platform/product-positioning problem;
- compatibility is linked to firms' positions rather than chosen first by national governments as standards policy;
- no international standards coalition or national-welfare stability game.

DOI: 10.1016/j.ijpe.2020.107779.

## 7. Barrett & Yang (2001) — international standards, network effects, redesign costs

Christopher B. Barrett and Yi-Nung Yang, *Rational incompatibility with international product standards*, Journal of International Economics 54(1), 171–191.

The model contains an exogenous international standard, network effects, redesign costs, firm deviation/compliance incentives, and government incentives not to enforce the international standard.

Threat:

- redesign/repositioning cost cannot be advertised as novel;
- national government and firm incentives can diverge around standardization;
- legacy technology/network effects can rationalize incompatibility.

Distance:

- international standard is an exogenous benchmark rather than a three-regime continuous government choice followed by horizontal product repositioning;
- no IS/SU/SW coalition-stability feedback from endogenous product locations.

DOI: 10.1016/S0022-1996(00)00082-9.

## 8. Brekke, Nuscheler & Straume (2006) — regulation can change endogenous locations

Kurt R. Brekke, Robert Nuscheler and Odd Rune Straume, *Quality and Location Choices under Price Regulation*, Journal of Economics & Management Strategy 15(1), 207–227.

A regulator commits to a policy before firms choose location and quality; regulation changes equilibrium horizontal differentiation.

Implication:

`regulator policy -> endogenous firm location` is generic prior art in spatial competition. C-ESD must be standards-specific and coalition-specific.

DOI: 10.1111/j.1530-9134.2006.00098.x.

## 9. Economides / standards-coalition family

Economides & Flyer and Economides & Skrzypacz establish that compatibility/network effects and standards-coalition formation already interact strongly with market structure and product differentiation.

They reinforce the rule that C-ESD cannot rely on the words `circle`, `compatibility`, `network`, or `coalition` as novelty claims.

## 10. Prior-art conclusion

The following broad claim is rejected:

> Government standardization causes firms to change product differentiation.

Ruiz and the spatial-regulation literature already make that type of statement available.

The following firm-side claim is also rejected:

> Network effects and compatibility interact with endogenous product positioning.

Baake–Boom and Wang–Lyu already occupy that space.

The residual C-ESD candidate is narrower:

> Under a three-country IS/SU/SW standards-partition game, a continuously chosen government standard-differentiation policy changes the compatibility-network topology faced by firms; the asymmetric SU topology then induces strategic product repositioning that is absent under symmetric IS/SW, and this continuation response changes national coalition participation/deviation incentives.

That full loop was not found in the targeted audit. But it is only a plausible novelty window, not a finding. The government policy map must first be frozen and the welfare/stability implication proved.

## 11. Referee attack to preserve

The strongest likely attack is:

> Ruiz already has standards policy followed by endogenous product characteristics, while Gandal–Shy already has three-country standards unions and network effects. C-ESD merely overlays two known architectures.

The only adequate answer would be a theorem showing that the **interaction** creates a government coalition-stability result unavailable in either benchmark separately. If Stage 4 cannot prove such a result, terminate C-ESD.
