\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

International standardization reduces technical incompatibility, expands network reach, and can lower adaptation costs. But standardization also changes the competitive environment faced by firms. When a regulated interface becomes more homogeneous, firms need not remain equally homogeneous along every other product dimension. They can reposition products, redesign features, or otherwise re-create horizontal differentiation outside the standardized interface. This paper asks whether that strategic response can change the political stability of international standards coalitions.

The question matters because standards policy is often neither purely binary nor purely technological. Governments can choose how deeply a common standard is implemented, while firms retain discretion over product attributes not fixed by the standard. A regional standards union can therefore have two effects at once: it compresses standard-based differentiation among member firms and increases relative separation from outsiders, while simultaneously changing member firms' incentives to differentiate on remaining product dimensions. Evaluating a standards coalition at fixed product characteristics can miss this continuation response.

We study a three-country model in which governments first form a formal standards regime---international standardization, a two-country standardization union, or a standards war. Each standards bloc then chooses the depth of integration. Deeper integration lowers pairwise standard friction inside the bloc and raises relative friction across bloc boundaries. Firms observe these policy choices, choose horizontal product locations on a Salop circle at a real redesign cost, and then compete in prices in the presence of network effects. National governments value consumer surplus and domestic producer profits, and coalition stability is evaluated using the regime-specific continuation equilibrium.

The main result is an interaction-induced coalition-stability reversal. We compare the full model with two mandatory benchmarks. In the first, governments choose standard depth but firm product locations are fixed. In the second, product locations are endogenous but standard depth is fixed at zero. At a verified regular parameter region, each benchmark selects international standardization. When both margins are endogenous, however, member firms of a regional standards union strategically move apart after internal standard friction is reduced. This re-differentiation raises domestic producer rents sufficiently to overturn the member-country welfare ranking. The regional union becomes stable and international standardization becomes pair-blockable.

The result is not a claim that the entire parameter space admits this ranking. The location game on a circle can exhibit order-changing deviations when redesign costs are too low. We therefore establish the result constructively on a regular nonempty region: the canonical witness satisfies all downstream regularity conditions and a whole-circle unilateral location-deviation test, the welfare inequalities are strict, and continuity gives an open neighborhood. Numerical audits confirm the same sign pattern across nearby parameter grids. The paper does not upgrade this constructive regular-region result into a global closed-form theorem.

The welfare mechanism is also distributional. For a prospective union member,
\[
\Delta_M=\Delta\Pi_M+\frac{\Delta CS}{3}.
\]
At the canonical witness, the member country's consumer-surplus component is negative, whereas the domestic-profit component is positive and slightly larger. The political attraction of the regional union therefore comes from domestic producer-rent capture rather than from a consumer gain. At the same witness, international standardization yields higher global welfare than the stable regional union because price transfers cancel globally and the remaining real terms favor the broader standard. The decentralized coalition game can thus regionalize standards even when world welfare favors international integration.

A second welfare comparison concerns product positioning itself. After the regional standard is deepened, a constrained social planner also chooses some re-differentiation, so the private response is not purely wasteful. Private firms nevertheless move farther apart than the constrained social benchmark. Standard integration therefore creates a margin on which strategic competition can partially undo the competitive consequences of the policy and can do so excessively from a social perspective.

The paper is related most closely to international standards policy and to endogenous product differentiation under compatibility. \citet{GandalShy2001} already analyze three-country standardization unions, network effects, national welfare, and coalition incentives. \citet{Ruiz2004} already allows governments to choose standards recognition before firms choose product characteristics and prices. \citet{Klimenko2009} studies continuous government compatibility policy with network externalities. These results rule out any contribution claim based merely on government compatibility policy, policy-induced firm adaptation, or partial standards unions. Our contribution is narrower: neither the policy-only nor the product-location-only benchmark overturns international standardization, while their interaction does so through strategic product re-differentiation. The relevant novelty is therefore the equilibrium feedback and the resulting coalition-ranking reversal, not the ingredients separately.
