\section{Welfare, Incidence, and Generality}\label{sec:welfare}

The coalition reversal is a national-welfare result, not a consumer-surplus result. This section separates the incidence driving government preferences from global welfare and from the private product-positioning distortion.

\subsection{National welfare and producer-rent incidence}

Integrating utility around the circle gives aggregate consumer surplus
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cs-identity}
CS=A+vq'G_\rho q-\sum_i p_iq_i-TC,
\end{equation}
where $TC$ is total standard-induced transportation/adaptation cost. Since each country contains one third of consumers,
\[
W_i=\frac{CS}{3}+\Pi_i.
\]
For a prospective $SU$ member, the welfare change relative to $IS$ is therefore exactly
\begin{equation}\label{eq:member-decomposition}
\Delta_M=\Delta\Pi_M+\frac{\Delta CS}{3}.
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}[Member welfare threshold]\label{prop:member-threshold}
A prospective member prefers a standardization union to international standardization if and only if
\begin{equation}\label{eq:member-threshold}
\Delta\Pi_M>-\frac{\Delta CS}{3}.
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

At the canonical witness, the consumer component is negative,
\[
\frac{\Delta CS}{3}=-0.0325785,
\]
whereas the domestic-profit component is positive,
\[
\Delta\Pi_M=0.0341498.
\]
Their sum is
\[
\Delta_M=0.0015713>0.
\]
Hence the regional union becomes politically attractive because the rise in domestic producer rents narrowly exceeds the member country's consumer loss. The model therefore generates a distributional wedge between national standards policy and consumer welfare.

\subsection{Global welfare}

Summing national welfare across countries eliminates price payments as transfers. Using \eqref{eq:cs-identity} and $\Pi_i=p_iq_i-C_i^D$,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:global-welfare}
GW=CS+\sum_i\Pi_i
=A+vq'G_\rho q-TC-\sum_iC_i^D.
\end{equation}
Thus only real network value, adaptation/transport losses, and redesign costs enter world welfare beyond the common baseline utility $A$.

At the canonical witness, the numerical levels are reported net of the common baseline $A$:
\[
GW_{IS}-A=-0.0225000,\qquad
GW_{SU}-A=-0.0586685,\qquad
GW_{SW}-A=-0.0700000.
\]
Accordingly, the decentralized coalition game selects regional unions even though international standardization yields higher global welfare at this witness.

\begin{proposition}[National--global welfare wedge at the canonical witness]\label{prop:global-wedge}
At the canonical witness, the stable decentralized regimes are the three two-country standardization unions, while global welfare is strictly higher under international standardization.
\end{proposition}

This proposition is witness-specific. We do not claim that $IS$ globally dominates $SU$ for every regular parameterization.

\subsection{Private versus social re-differentiation}

The same mechanism generates a second distortion in product space. Hold the canonical $SU_{12}$ member-bloc harmonization depth fixed at $s_{12}=0.25$; outsider country 3 is a singleton and therefore has $s_3=0$ by definition. The inherited member distance is
\[
D^0=0.333333.
\]
A constrained social planner that chooses product locations while taking the standards policy as fixed selects
\[
D^{SP}=0.431427,
\]
whereas private firms choose
\[
D^P=0.497533.
\]
Hence
\[
D^P>D^{SP}>D^0.
\]
Some re-differentiation is socially useful after standard integration, but private firms re-differentiate more than the constrained social benchmark because they internalize the competition-softening effect on their own profits rather than the full consumer cost.

Again, this comparison is a verified property of the canonical witness, not a global theorem.

\subsection{Adjustment costs and the regular region}

The quadratic redesign cost in \eqref{eq:reposition-cost} provides a transparent baseline and a linear conditional location system. The economic mechanism does not rely on quadratic curvature per se. For a regular differentiable strictly convex cost $C(d)$ with $C(0)=C'(0)=0$, a nonzero outward marginal operating-profit force generated by the $SU$ asymmetry still induces positive re-differentiation whenever it can be balanced by $C'(d)$.

The important restriction is intermediate effective adjustment-cost curvature. If adjustment costs are very high, firms respond too little for the domestic rent gain to overturn the consumer loss. If adjustment costs are sufficiently low, order-changing deviations may invalidate the selected fixed-order branch, so the global continuation must be checked directly. At $v=0.04$ and $\bar s=0.25$, the verified upper welfare threshold is
\[
\gamma_W=0.132983.
\]
For the canonical value $\gamma=0.11$, the Stage-4R verification directly checks whole-circle continuation validity over the full feasible repaired policy domain. We do not report a structural closed-form lower $\gamma$ threshold for global-best-response validity.

\subsection{Institutional interpretation}

The policy primitive is intended to represent the depth of a regulated interface or interoperability standard rather than an all-encompassing product design. In the model, $s_C$ is specifically the additional harmonization depth implemented inside an already formed non-singleton standards coalition; a singleton country has no internal harmonization margin. Examples include common charging interfaces, charging-network interoperability rules, and digital messaging interoperability obligations. Such policies can compress differentiation on a standardized interface while leaving firms free to differentiate on other characteristics. The model's empirical prediction is therefore not merely that interoperability changes prices; it is that partial or regional standardization can be followed by stronger differentiation on non-standardized product dimensions.

The institutional examples motivate the policy-controlled interoperability margin. They do not establish that strategic re-differentiation has already occurred empirically in those settings. That response remains a falsifiable implication of the model.
