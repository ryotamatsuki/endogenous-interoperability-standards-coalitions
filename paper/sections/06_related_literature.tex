\section{Related Literature}\label{sec:literature}

This paper sits at the intersection of three established literatures: international standards policy, compatibility and network competition, and endogenous product differentiation. Its contribution is not any one of these ingredients, but the interaction among them in the coalition-stability problem.

The closest benchmark for the international-policy architecture is \citet{GandalShy2001}. They study standardization policy in a three-country environment with network effects and standardization unions, and show that partial standardization can be relevant for national welfare and coalition incentives. Our model deliberately retains this type of international-regime comparison but adds a continuous bloc-depth choice and, crucially, a post-policy product-positioning stage. The paper therefore does not claim novelty for the existence or possible stability of a standardization union itself.

The closest benchmark for the policy-to-product-characteristic timing is \citet{Ruiz2004}. In that analysis, governments choose standard-recognition policy before firms choose product characteristics and prices. This makes clear that strategic product adaptation to standards policy is not new. The distinction here is result-level: the endogenous product response interacts with regime-specific standard depth strongly enough to reverse the government coalition ranking relative to both a policy-only and a location-only benchmark.

Continuous government compatibility policy in network industries is also well established. \citet{Klimenko2009} studies technical-compatibility policies and international agreements in industries with network externalities. We therefore do not treat a continuous compatibility margin as a contribution in itself. What matters here is that the policy-induced change in compatibility alters a separate horizontal product-positioning incentive before governments' coalition values are evaluated.

A broader industrial-organization literature studies compatibility, network externalities, and endogenous differentiation on the firm side. \citet{BaakeBoom2001} analyze product differentiation, network externalities, and compatibility decisions; \citet{MatutesPadilla1994} show how partial network sharing can arise in a spatial banking environment; and \citet{DingKoShen2022} characterize partial compatibility and welfare in two-sided markets. These papers establish that compatibility can trade off network gains against competitive insulation and that partial compatibility may emerge endogenously. Our result differs in the identity of the decision maker and the strategic feedback: national governments choose standards depth, firms subsequently re-differentiate horizontally, and that firm response changes the stability of an international standards coalition.

Accordingly, the narrow contribution is the following. Existing work separately provides government standards policy, endogenous firm adaptation, network effects, and partial compatibility or standards unions. We identify a regular parameter region in which the policy margin alone selects international standardization, the product-location margin alone also selects international standardization, but their joint endogeneity induces member firms to re-differentiate and makes regional standards unions stable. The paper should therefore be read as an interaction result, not as a claim of a new compatibility technology or a new coalition concept.
