\section{Related Literature}\label{sec:literature}

This paper sits at the intersection of international standards policy, compatibility and network competition, and endogenous product differentiation. The contribution is not any one of these ingredients. It is the result-level consequence of allowing firms to reposition after a standards architecture is set: the induced product-market response can reverse governments' ranking of international standardization and a regional standards union relative to a fixed-position evaluation.

The closest benchmark for the international-policy architecture is \citet{GandalShy2001}. They study standardization policy in a three-country environment with network effects and standardization unions, and show that partial standardization can be relevant for national welfare and coalition incentives. Their analysis already establishes that a regional standards union can be politically relevant, so the present paper does not claim novelty for regional standards coalitions, national-welfare participation incentives, or the global-versus-national wedge.

The closest benchmark for the policy-to-product-characteristic timing is \citet{Ruiz2004}. In that analysis, governments choose standard-recognition policy before firms choose product characteristics and prices, and endogenous differentiation can be strategically excessive. This removes any novelty claim based simply on policy-induced firm adaptation. The distinction here is narrower. Ruiz's endogenous-characteristics extension preserves its qualitative government-policy result, whereas in our model the post-policy product-position response can reverse the member-country ranking between international standardization and a regional standards union relative to a fixed-position benchmark.

The repaired policy architecture also has close precedents in the regional-versus-multilateral standards literature. \citet{TakaradaEtAl2020} study three countries with endogenous standards, regional and multilateral harmonization, national welfare, and core blocking. A regional standards agreement can be the only regime in the core even when multilateral harmonization maximizes world welfare. \citet{KawabataTakarada2021} likewise analyze deep trade agreements and harmonization of standards. These papers make clear that continuous coalition-level harmonization, regional-versus-multilateral standards choice, and blocking are not setup contributions here. What they do not contain is a separate horizontal product-positioning stage after standards policy through which firms strategically re-differentiate and thereby change the government coalition ranking.

Continuous government compatibility policy in network industries is also well established. \citet{Klimenko2009} studies technical-compatibility policies and international agreements in industries with network externalities. A broader industrial-organization literature studies compatibility, network externalities, and endogenous differentiation on the firm side. \citet{BaakeBoom2001} analyze product differentiation, network externalities, and compatibility decisions; \citet{MatutesPadilla1994} show how partial network sharing can arise in a spatial banking environment; and \citet{DingKoShen2022} characterize partial compatibility and welfare in two-sided markets. These papers establish the familiar tradeoff between network gains and competitive insulation. The present mechanism instead asks how a government standards architecture changes a subsequent, separate product-positioning choice and thereby feeds back into national coalition incentives.

Recent interoperability work further narrows the claim. \citet{HuangEtAl2026} study weighted industry-wide and coalitional interoperability, pricing, profits, consumer surplus, and total welfare, but do not analyze endogenous government coalition formation with post-policy horizontal repositioning. \citet{KretschmerEtAl2025} show that firms can strategically respond to mandated interoperability through data collection and privacy spillovers. Thus generic strategic response to interoperability regulation is not a contribution of this paper either.

The strongest hostile reconstruction combines \citet{GandalShy2001}, which supplies the three-country standards-union and national-welfare coalition architecture, with \citet{Ruiz2004}, which supplies government standards policy followed by endogenous product characteristics, and \citet{TakaradaEtAl2020}, which supplies regional-versus-multilateral standards harmonization and blocking. That synthesis makes the setup unsurprising. The residual contribution is therefore deliberately stated at result level: holding the standards architecture fixed, allowing firms to reposition on a separate horizontal product dimension after policy can reverse member countries' $IS$--$SU$ welfare ranking relative to fixed product positions, making the regional union stable through producer-rent gains. At the canonical witness, fixing harmonization depths exogenously at their full-game values reproduces the reversal, so the paper does not claim that endogenous harmonization choice is necessary.

No priority claim is intended. The relevant statement is only that the audited closest literature does not directly establish this benchmarked post-policy repositioning reversal as a proposition or immediate corollary.
