\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper studies standards coalitions when firms can reposition products after the standards architecture is determined. The central mechanism is strategic re-differentiation. A regional standards union compresses standard-based differentiation between its members, but those member firms can respond by moving apart on another horizontal product dimension. That continuation response can soften competition and raise domestic producer rents enough to reverse the member-country welfare ranking relative to an evaluation that holds product positions fixed.

The benchmark structure is important for interpretation. In B-T, product positions remain inherited and international standardization is preferred at the canonical regular region. In FULL, firms reposition after policy and the regional union becomes nationally preferred and stable. Benchmark B-EQ fixes harmonization depths exogenously at their full-game values while retaining endogenous product positions; at the canonical witness it reproduces FULL. The implication is therefore not that endogenous harmonization choice is necessary. The identified margin is the post-policy firm response.

The welfare analysis shows why political and global rankings can diverge. The member-country gain is driven by domestic producer rents that outweigh a consumer-surplus loss, while price transfers cancel in world welfare. At the canonical witness, the regional union is politically stable even though international standardization yields higher global welfare. Firms also re-differentiate more than a constrained social planner after regional integration is fixed.

These results do not imply that regional standardization is always inefficient, that all interoperability policies induce re-differentiation, or that the result is general across alternative demand systems and timing structures. The headline reversal is a constructive result on a verified regular region, and whole-circle continuation validity is established computationally at the canonical primitives over the repaired feasible policy domain rather than by a global analytic theorem.

The analysis therefore identifies a limitation of fixed-product evaluations of standards policy. Once firms can reposition after harmonization, the induced change in product-market competition can shift surplus toward domestic producers enough to alter governments' preferred standards coalition. In the model, this makes a regional union nationally stable even though its members' consumers lose relative to international standardization and global welfare is lower. The policy implication is not that harmonization-depth endogeneity itself drives the reversal, but that post-policy product redesign can materially change coalition incentives.
